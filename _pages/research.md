# Research

We study genome evolution, particularly the origins and consequences of changes in genome content, organisation and sequence. Our questions span whole-genome duplication in cancer, the relationship between development and malignancy, endogenous viral sequences, and the evolution of RNA-processing machinery. We use cancer genome sequencing data, evolutionary inference and comparative genomics, and are interested in what new genomic technologies can reveal about these processes.

## Ploidy and genome evolution

<!-- ICON 1: Upload ploidy.svg to images/research-icons/ and uncomment the next line. -->
<!-- <img src="{{ '/images/research-icons/ploidy.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Abstract icon representing ploidy and genome evolution" width="120" height="120" style="display:block; margin: 12px 0 20px;"> -->

**Why do some cancers duplicate their genomes, while others survive extensive chromosome loss?**

Whole-genome duplication (WGD) can profoundly alter the course of cancer evolution. We want to understand when genome doubling occurs, how it changes subsequent evolutionary trajectories, and what determines which karyotypes persist. Our recent analysis of hypodiploid cancers revealed contrasting patterns of chromosome loss, from relatively stable karyotypes to pervasive chromosomal instability. We are now interested in the constraints on genome content and the interplay between WGD, chromosome loss and selection.

*Approaches:* whole-genome sequencing, allele-specific copy-number analysis, genomic event timing and evolutionary modelling.

## Developmental origins of cancer

<!-- ICON 2: Upload development.svg to images/research-icons/ and uncomment the next line. -->
<!-- <img src="{{ '/images/research-icons/development.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Abstract icon representing developmental cell lineages" width="120" height="120" style="display:block; margin: 12px 0 20px;"> -->

**What can cancer genomes tell us about normal development—and how does developmental history shape cancer?**

We study cancers whose biology is closely tied to development, including testicular germ cell tumours and gestational trophoblastic disease. What can their genomes tell us about the cells and developmental processes from which they arise? How do features of germ-cell and trophoblast biology shape the routes to malignancy? Our work tracing the *in utero* origin of myelofibrosis in monozygotic twins illustrates how somatic mutations can also provide a record of otherwise inaccessible events in early human development.

*Approaches:* cancer genome sequencing, somatic mutation analysis and reconstruction of developmental and tumour histories.

## Endogenous viral elements and genome evolution

<!-- ICON 3: Upload viral-elements.svg to images/research-icons/ and uncomment the next line. -->
<!-- <img src="{{ '/images/research-icons/viral-elements.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Abstract icon representing endogenous viral sequences in a genome" width="120" height="120" style="display:block; margin: 12px 0 20px;"> -->

**What can the viral sequences embedded in genomes tell us about evolution?**

Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) and other endogenous viral elements (EVEs) preserve traces of past interactions between viruses and their hosts. We are interested in when these sequences entered genomes, how they have diversified or been lost, and what their distribution reveals about genome evolution. This is an emerging research direction, with opportunities arising from improved genome assemblies, comparative datasets and new sequencing technologies.

*Potential approaches:* comparative genome analysis, phylogenetics and sequence-based computational methods.

## Evolution of RNA processing

<!-- ICON 4: Upload rna-processing.svg to images/research-icons/ and uncomment the next line. -->
<!-- <img src="{{ '/images/research-icons/rna-processing.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Abstract icon representing an RNA stem-loop" width="120" height="120" style="display:block; margin: 12px 0 20px;"> -->

**How do molecular pathways evolve—and how often do apparent evolutionary exceptions reflect gaps in what we have sampled?**

Replication-dependent histone mRNAs in animals typically undergo a specialised form of 3′-end processing rather than conventional polyadenylation. In recent work, we investigated the evolutionary distribution of the machinery responsible for this process across eukaryotes, identifying components in lineages where they had not previously been recognised. This raises new questions about the origins of the pathway and transitions between different modes of histone mRNA processing.

*Approaches:* comparative genomics, sequence homology searches, protein structural analysis and mapping of RNA-processing features.
